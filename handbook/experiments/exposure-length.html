10, 20 or 30 seconds? Two filters, one Seestar.

Does a longer sub help under a darker narrowband sky, or does tracking loss still dominate?

In these tested stacks, 20 s has the lowest measured stack noise on both targets. M 33 has no clear depth winner after approximate smoothing; a small edge for 20 s cannot be excluded. The LP result makes the cost of short subs more visible, but does not establish its cause.

The question and the rig

Jeff used one Seestar S50 in EQ mode at gain 80, at one site. Each target was observed over 3 nights, rotating exposure order so each setting took each slot once.

M 33 used IR-cut; NGC 281 used the LP dual-narrowband Hα + OIII filter. The targets and nights differ, so this is a comparison of two field tests, not a controlled filter-only experiment.

The same script lineage was used: equal submitted time for timing, then equal registered integration for depth, a shared star mask and flux matching on the reference grid. Integration targets follow each dataset’s available counts.

Evidence section: Method and source precedence

What the S50 throws away

Never-saved counts are inferred from cadence gaps after allowing for overhead and dithering. Saved registration failures are measured on the equal-submitted-time timing subsets; their survival rates are extrapolated to all saved subs to estimate usable minutes per clock hour. These are different denominators.

The charts combine those stages as estimated fractions of saved plus inferred missing frames. Clock coverage is first-to-last saved timestamp plus one exposure for each block. Small rate differences have no uncertainty estimate.

Evidence section: Equal registered integration

Does star size explain the LP result?

The LP short-sub deficit remains across the tested blur bracket and stronger smoothing. The first trial remains narrower than the reference; the next is wider. This brackets the reference size, rather than proving exact PSF equivalence. The fine-scale deficit is not explained by star-size difference in these trials.

Larger noise bands rise with blur, so they are not used as matched comparisons. The longest-sub stack was not PSF-matched; its stack-level noise gap remains unexplained. Trailed survivors and wider stars are untested candidates.

For M 33, approximately smoothed short-sub FWHM is 2.899 px versus 2.975 px for the reference; matching is approximate there too.

The cost of usable time

Processing is register plus stack wall time divided by the original timing run’s actual registered integration, not the depth stack duration. Storage includes saved frames that later fail registration, and uses the selected survival extrapolation.

Storage assumes 4.15 decimal MB per saved frame; it is an estimate, not a raw-byte audit. Hardware-specific timing is not a universal benchmark.

Evidence section: Cost formulas

What it means

On LP, the shortest subs are about eleven percent noisier at the tested fine scale. This is consistent with a read-noise penalty under the darker sky; read noise is a hypothesis, not a finding. No bias/dark calibration established causation.

The longest-sub stack-level gap is unexplained. Shorter subs retain more frames and rounder stars; the reference exposure balances measured depth, usable time and storage in these field tests. These results do not prescribe one exposure for every sky, target or mount.

Evidence section: Interpretation and limits

What we cannot claim

  • 3 nights per target; one stack per exposure and no uncertainty estimate.
  • Partial PSF matching; the longest LP subs are unmatched. No bias/dark calibration.
  • Different targets and nights confound a filter-only causal comparison.
  • Sky samples are 71.85% for M 33 and 65.59% for NGC 281. Retained footprint is not star-mask coverage.
